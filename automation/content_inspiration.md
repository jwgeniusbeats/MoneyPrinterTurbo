# Content inspiration

Running list of genuinely good psychology/facts content formats seen on other
accounts during engagement rounds (liking/following/commenting). Not
competitor data dumped wholesale -- just formats/angles worth adapting in our
own words for future `automation/topic_backlog.json` entries or
`daily_pipeline.py` prompt inspiration. Standing practice every engagement
round, not a one-off: log a new bullet whenever something genuinely reusable
is spotted, skip logging if nothing new this round.

## Formats worth stealing (structure, not the content itself)

- "X things humans physically can't do (and why)" — @neuromatrix_, 43.8M likes. Numbered list of surprising body/brain limitations, each with a one-line neuroscience reason. Very shareable, matches our own style (short reason per item).
- "N brain facts so you can fight brain rot" — @emersynrrose. Frames a facts-listicle as a weekly self-improvement roundup rather than a bare list — gives it a personal, ongoing-series feel instead of a one-off.
- "How to know if someone is lying to you, instantly" — @psych.scenes (130K followers). Direct practical-skill framing (you can DO something with this fact) outperforms pure trivia framing.
- "6 psychology tricks that make you instantly more powerful" — @ellvado. Numbered + "instantly" + power/confidence framing; strong for anything about persuasion/confidence topics.
- Consistent visual branding across posts (a recurring motif, e.g. @psych.index's eye imagery on every thumbnail) — makes an account instantly recognizable in a feed/grid even before reading the title. We already have the brain-lightbulb logo; worth keeping it in every title-card background/overlay consistently rather than varying stock footage styles wildly.
- "Limits aren't weaknesses. They're safeguards." style closing line (@neuromatrix_) — a short reframing punchline at the end lands well and is very close to what FOLLOW_CTA_INSTRUCTION already tries to do naturally.
- "That urge to squeeze/bite your partner isn't violence, it's biology — Evolutionary psychology calls this 'Cute Aggression'" — @thebluebulb, 2M likes, 15.2K comments. Names an obscure real psychology term for a universally-relatable everyday feeling, then explains it in one line. Top comments were people riffing with their own funny examples — naming-a-feeling format is strong bait for quotable/funny replies, which drives comment count.
- "5 Questions that can Hijack [someone's] Brain" — @mindshadowlabs (39.9K followers, bio: "I teach you what others hide. Dark Psychology • Silent Tactics • Social Power"). Numbered-questions-as-hook format, framed as secret/hidden knowledge. "Dark psychology" framing (manipulation-awareness angle) draws heavy engagement in this niche — worth an occasional topic in that vein (e.g. "signs someone is manipulating you"), kept ethical/defensive in framing rather than teaching manipulation.
- "How to Trick Your Brain Into Doing Hard Things" — @carlytalks2, 53K likes, 516 comments, unusually long-form (7:22, not a typical short). Top comment (744 likes) distilled the whole video into a memorable acronym: **LOTUS** — Lower the difficulty, Outsmart yourself, Ten minutes only, Use rewards, Stack small wins. Acronym-of-the-technique format is very shareable/saveable on its own — worth building a script around a coined acronym when a topic has ~5 discrete steps, rather than just listing them.
- "Strong Empathy" / "A Cluttered Desk" / "Sign Of Creativity" — Untold Facts (Facebook, 66K likes/post). Simple hand-drawn stick-figure whiteboard-style animation illustrating one personality/behavior trait per short clip, bold yellow-highlighted keyword in the caption text overlay. Very different visual language from our stock-footage style but performs strongly -- worth considering an occasional simple-illustration or animated-text-only video as a format experiment, not a wholesale switch.
- Poetic/reflective micro-essay captions (not just a single explainer line) — @robthebank (Instagram, 1M likes on one post about emotional intelligence/forgiveness). Short punchy paragraphs building to a quotable closing line ("That's not cold. That's clarity."). Contrasts with our current punchy-single-fact captions; worth trying this longer, more reflective caption style on an occasional post about relationships/boundaries/emotional topics rather than pure trivia.

- "Why do people suddenly stop trying, even when success is right in front of them?" — @reflectraexplores (Instagram), 1.7M likes. Names the concept (Learned Helplessness) then explains it entirely through one vivid experiment narrative (predator fish hits an invisible barrier repeatedly, stops trying, then never attacks again even after the barrier is removed) instead of listing facts. The story structure -- setup, repeated failure, removed obstacle, lasting effect -- does the explaining; no bullet points needed. Strong template for any single-concept video: pick one real experiment/demonstration and narrate it start to finish rather than summarizing the concept abstractly.
- Evidence-backed hook with real citations — @shadezahrai (Instagram), verified account. "Your brain edits reality. Neuroscience calls it top-down processing" followed by a plain-language mechanism and then actual citations in parentheses (e.g. "Balcetis & Dunning, 2006"). Citing real researchers/years (even briefly, not as a wall of references) adds credibility in this niche and reads as more authoritative than uncited claims -- worth doing occasionally when a fact traces to a specific named study.
- "Two mirrors" before/after narrative device — @reflectraexplores (Instagram), 195+ likes, on a Christian Bale/The Machinist post. Structure: "He stood in a mirror and watched a stranger stare back... Less than a year later, he stood in another mirror. The stranger was still there. This time made of muscle, still not himself." Same framing device (a mirror moment) repeated at two extremes, closing on a line about what *didn't* change (identity) despite what did (the body). Reusable template for any transformation/willpower/identity topic -- bookend the video with the same visual or phrase at two different states rather than just narrating change linearly.
- Reframing an absence as a deliberate choice — @psychovius (Instagram), psychology-facts account. "Why Some People Don't Post Their Life" explains not-posting not as lacking something to share but as a sign of internal vs. external validation, closing with "The absence of visibility does not mean the absence of life." Works because it validates a behavior the viewer may feel defensive about, then reframes it as a strength -- a reusable angle for any "why don't people do X" topic (e.g. why some people don't ask for help, don't share feelings) rather than always explaining why people DO something.

- "Care Less List / Care More List" — @shadezahrai (Instagram) x Mel Robbins collab, confidence/self-acceptance tool. Concrete two-list exercise: write down everything you want to care less about (appearance, others' opinions, family comments), then a second list of what to care more about (growth, showing up, your values) — reframed as noticing thought patterns keeping you stuck and redirecting mental energy. A specific, doable exercise (not just a fact) gives viewers something to actually act on, which tends to drive saves/shares — worth an occasional "two contrasting lists" format for any self-improvement topic (e.g. Fear Less / Fear More, Control List / Release List).
- The "reliability trap" career-psychology narrative — @shadezahrai (Instagram), first-person account: "When I worked in corporate, I was the reliable one... I watched people who weren't necessarily working harder get recognized faster because they had time to be visible, while I was busy doing all the extra things I'd been given because I was 'so reliable.'" Names a specific, relatable trap (being so consistently competent that you become invisible for advancement) via personal story rather than a listicle — strong template for any "hidden cost of a strength" topic (e.g. the cost of being the funny one, the cost of never asking for help).
- Optical-illusion-as-life-metaphor — @psychology_facts (Instagram), old but evergreen post (4.5K+ likes): three photos that individually look like a dog/rabbit/cat, but zoomed out together reveal a couple embracing. Caption ties it directly to a life lesson: "That is how life is, until you zoom on people's lives you wouldn't understand who they really are." Reusable device — pair any perception/illusion visual with a one-line reframe about judging people or situations without full context.

- Nervous-system reframe for isolation/burnout — @psychovius (Instagram), 102 days, 10K+ likes. "The more you isolate, the more your nervous system adapts to stillness... leaving feels exhausting, motivation drops... you think you're resting, but your nervous system is actually shutting down stimulation... sometimes the cure isn't more rest, it's gentle re-engagement." Reframes a common self-help cliché (rest/self-care) as sometimes the wrong prescription, using a nervous-system-adaptation mechanism instead of pure willpower framing. Reusable template for any "why doing the healthy-sounding thing isn't helping" topic.

- "5 Types of Procrastination and the Cure" -- @successtheory.co (IG). Instead of one universal tip, splits a problem into named subtypes (e.g. different procrastination patterns) then pairs each with its own specific fix. A diagnose-your-type-first structure reads as more personalized/credible than a single one-size-fits-all list, and gives a natural reason to comment ("which type are you"). Reusable for any topic with several distinct underlying causes (e.g. types of overthinking, types of people-pleasing) rather than one root cause.

- "Only 3% of People Notice These Subtle Behaviour Patterns (Psychology Explains Why)" -- @ThinkVeil (TikTok), 18.1K likes, 4343 shares. Percentage-exclusivity framing ("only X% notice/do Y") makes an ordinary observation feel like insider knowledge, stronger hook than a plain "signs of X" list title. Reusable title pattern for any noticing/awareness topic.

Note (2026-09-23): @successtheory.co (IG) is active again with frequent new carousels (contradicts the "posting has slowed" note from 2026-09-20) -- worth checking every round again. It now cross-posts everything through @insightful.theory as a second co-branded account (same content, same posting cadence) -- following/liking on either counts toward the same creator, so treat them as one source rather than two for topic-inspiration purposes, though following the second account is still fine for reach.

- "The fighter pilot rule for when you overthink any decision" -- @emersynrrose (IG). Borrows an authority/discipline framing (a military decision-making heuristic) to justify a simple act-now-perfect-later message, rather than presenting the advice as generic self-help. Caption: "No need to overthink anymore. Act now, perfection comes later." Reusable pattern: attach an everyday piece of advice to a specific outside profession/discipline's "rule" to make it feel earned rather than obvious.
